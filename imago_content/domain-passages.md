# Domain Passages

Every person who takes the IMAGO assessment receives five domain scores. Those scores describe the shape of your wiring across five big arenas of human personality: Imagination, Mastery, Animation, Grace-bearing, and Ortho-emotion. That's the I-M-A-G-O.

For each domain, you'll receive one passage — either the HIGH pole passage or the LOW pole passage, matched to where your score actually landed. Both poles are image-bearing. Neither pole is closer to God or more useful to the Church. Each carries a gift and a shadow. Each has a calling the Spirit wants to develop in you.

You will not receive both passages — only the one that is yours. Read it slowly. It is describing you.

---

## I — Imagination

### HIGH Imagination

> **Imagination — HIGH (at or above the 60th percentile)**
> Imagination is your wiring for ideas, beauty, and the pull toward what hasn't been tried yet.
> Biblical anchor: "Eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor have entered into the heart of man the things which God has prepared for those who love him. But God has revealed them to us through his Spirit." (1 Corinthians 2:9–10)

You are in the middle of a conversation at dinner and someone says something — a half-formed observation, a strange question — and you feel it catch, the way a fire catches on a new piece of wood. Everyone else moves on. You can't. You are still turning the idea over three hours later when the dishes are done and the house is quiet. That's you. That has always been you.

**What this wiring looks like**

You reach toward what is new, not just as a preference but as a reflex. A blank page excites you more than a checklist. The unmapped territory pulls on you in a way the well-worn path simply doesn't. This shows up in the sensory world — you notice the light coming through the window at a particular angle; a piece of music can stop you mid-step; you see something in an ordinary object that others walk past without registering. And it shows up in the intellectual world — you follow ideas wherever they lead, even off the edge of the map. Your mind asks "what if?" before it asks "how?"

Think of the person who, sitting in a routine staff meeting, is already three hypothetical steps ahead. Not because they're bored exactly, but because their mind won't stay still. Or the one who, while everyone else is watching the film, is quietly taking it apart and rebuilding it into something else. That movement from what is to what could be is your native gear.

People around you probably describe you as creative, or insightful, or as someone who's hard to predict. What they're noticing is real: your natural orientation toward what hasn't been fully mapped yet.

**The imago Dei connection**

The opening pages of Scripture show us a God who creates. He speaks and light appears, and then he doesn't stop — he makes variation, depth, color, texture, complexity. He names things. He delights in what he has made. And then he makes you, in his image, with some measure of that same delight in making and in seeing. Your Imagination is not a secular trait the Church should be nervous about. It is the echo in you of a Creator who did not make a gray world.

Bezalel in Exodus 31 was filled with the Spirit of God specifically to create — "with skill, with intelligence, with knowledge, and with all craftsmanship." He was given artistry as a calling, not as a distraction from holiness. When you follow an idea into a new room, you are doing something the image of God in you was always meant to do.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to consecrate your restlessness. Isaiah 50:4 describes the "tongue of the learned" given to "speak a word in season to him who is weary." The images God sends through minds like yours are not only for your own delight. They are for the body. What you see that others don't — what you understand before anyone has figured out how to say it — that was given to you for the sake of others.

The practical challenge is this: ideas are cleaner than people. A half-developed concept doesn't push back or get tired or need you on a Tuesday when you'd rather be thinking. Your calling is to bring your explorations home — to let the beauty and insight you gather do its work in the lives around you. Thomas à Kempis warned against "learning without charity." For you, the antidote is not less curiosity. It is curiosity set loose in love.

**The shadow for your pole**

High Imagination has a characteristic shadow: the next idea can quietly erode your fidelity to the present one. It is always more interesting to begin something new than to finish something hard. Curiosity can become a subtle form of escape from the demands of ordinary faithfulness — the friend who needs you to stay, the commitment that required more than you thought, the idea you half-launched and then left when a better one appeared.

There is also a lonelier shadow. When your way of seeing the world feels isolating — when others don't follow you into the territory you find so obvious — the temptation is to withdraw into the interior life and slowly stop offering what you carry. David Powlison observed in <i>Seeing with New Eyes</i> that our gifts, when unsubmitted, tend to become our hiding places. The call is to stay in the room. To offer what you see, even when it isn't immediately understood.

**One gospel anchor**

"Eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor have entered into the heart of man the things which God has prepared for those who love him. But God has revealed them to us through his Spirit." (1 Corinthians 2:9–10)

Your capacity for wonder is not the ceiling — it is a window. The God who made you restless made you so because what he is preparing cannot fit inside a comfortable mind. Your exploration is not aimless. You are being drawn forward by the One whose imagination made yours.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that you make the ordinary luminous — that you find the strange angle on the familiar thing, that you ask the question no one else thought to ask. They may also notice that in harder seasons you can disappear into your own interior world, and from the outside that looks like a closed door. The gift and the shadow come from the same source. Your partner needs what you see. They also need you to stay present long enough to share it.

### LOW Imagination

> **Imagination — LOW (below the 60th percentile)**
> Imagination is your wiring for ideas and novelty — and your wiring runs closer to the ground, toward the real, the reliable, and the concrete.
> Biblical anchor: "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them." (Ephesians 2:10)

Someone puts a new plan on the table at the church meeting. Everyone starts riffing. The energy in the room picks up, ideas are bouncing, someone writes half-finished sentences on a whiteboard. You listen. And then, when the excitement has peaked and started to thin, you ask the question that clears the room: "Who is actually going to do this?" Not because you want to dampen things. Because it's the right question and someone has to ask it. That's you.

**What this wiring looks like**

You are not primarily a reacher toward the new. You're someone for whom the familiar, the established, the concrete, and the proven have genuine and deep value. Where others need novelty to feel alive, you need solidity. You go deep in what you know rather than spread thin across what you don't. When someone lays out a theory, your first instinct is to find out what it means in practice — what the next step is, who does it, and when.

Think of the person who volunteers to run the same Sunday school class for twelve years and becomes the best teacher in the building, because depth of repetition produced mastery. Or the one at the work project who reads the whole manual — while everyone else has already moved on — and who, three weeks later, solves the problem no one else could solve because they actually knew what the manual said. That patience with the reliable and the real is your gift.

In a world full of starters, you are someone who finishes. That is rarer than people think.

**The imago Dei connection**

The God who creates is also the God who sustains — "upholding all things by the word of his power" (Hebrews 1:3). Creation is not just the first act; it is the ongoing act of faithful presence to what exists. And when Jesus came, he didn't arrive as an abstract theorist. He came and touched lepers. He made mud for blind eyes. He called fishermen to nets they already understood. The incarnation is the ultimate act of radical concreteness — God inhabiting the material and the ordinary.

Nehemiah didn't stand at a distance and envision the wall. He went out at night and walked the rubble himself, measured what was broken, organized the workers by section, and kept careful records of who was building where (Nehemiah 3). Your temperament runs in that same direction: faithful stewardship of the actual, specific, real thing in front of you.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to trust that the concrete faithfulness of your life is not small. 1 Corinthians 12 insists that the body of Christ is not made of spectacle alone. It is made of the patient, reliable members who hold things together while the visionaries have moved on. Your calling is to stay — to be the one who finishes what others begin, who applies what others theorize, who makes the abstract livable.

There is also a secondary invitation here: not to become someone else, but to let the unfamiliar have a word occasionally. Ruth walked into a world she did not know — "wherever you go, I will go" (Ruth 1:16). That wasn't a betrayal of her loyal nature; it was the full expression of it in new territory. Sometimes the most faithful thing you can do is follow someone into an idea you haven't tested yet.

**The shadow for your pole**

The shadow of your wiring is not stupidity or small-mindedness. It is the slow hardening of the familiar into the ultimate — the quiet drift from "I prefer what is proven" to "I am suspicious of what is new." Tradition becomes traditionalism. Prudence becomes resistance. What started as a healthy preference for the concrete becomes a subtle contempt for those who see differently.

Powlison names this pattern in <i>Making All Things New</i>: when legitimate preferences become demands, when what is good for us becomes the standard for everyone, we've crossed from gift into idol. The specific call for you is to make room for those whose imaginations see what yours does not. The visionary in your marriage, your small group, your team — they're not irresponsible. They're carrying something you need.

**One gospel anchor**

"For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them." (Ephesians 2:10)

The good works God prepared for you are not abstract. They are specific, embodied, particular. Your orientation toward the concrete and the practical is not a secondary mode of serving God. It is exactly the mode in which much of the Kingdom gets built.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that you show love in concrete and reliable ways — that your word means something, that you remember what matters, that you are still there when the excitement has worn off. They may also notice that you can be slow to follow them into unfamiliar territory, whether that's an idea, an experience, or an emotion they're asking you to sit with. What they need is not for you to love novelty. What they need is your steady presence offering them a reason to come home.

## M — Mastery

### HIGH Mastery

> **Mastery — HIGH (at or above the 60th percentile)**
> Mastery is your wiring for effort, follow-through, and the faithful stewardship of what you've been given to do.
> Biblical anchor: "Well done, good and faithful servant. You have been faithful over a little; I will set you over much." (Matthew 25:21)

There's a kind of person who, when they say they'll have something done by Thursday, has it done by Wednesday. Not to impress anyone. Not because they're anxious about being late. Just because when they say a thing, they mean it, and meaning it means doing it. That's you. You probably have always been that person. It may not even feel remarkable to you — it just feels like basic decency. But the people around you have noticed. And they count on it.

**What this wiring looks like**

You finish what you start. When others trail off, you close. When others are still warming up to a task, you've already mapped the steps. Commitments aren't casual for you — you feel their weight, and you don't give them carelessly. You tend toward order in your time, your space, your finances, your plans — not because you're rigid, but because order is the medium through which your best work happens.

Think of the person who volunteers to organize the church pantry and, three months later, has labeled containers, a rotation system, a volunteer schedule, and a budget spreadsheet — and who did it all quietly, without making it about themselves. Or the one who takes on a work project that no one else wanted and has it not only finished but documented, so the next person who touches it will know exactly what to do. That thoroughness is your gift. It creates things that last.

You probably feel something close to discomfort when commitments are broken, when work is left half-done, when preparation is careless. That discomfort is information: it tells you what you're made for.

**The imago Dei connection**

"God finished his work" (Genesis 2:2). He didn't abandon creation halfway through — he worked through the full arc of what he had begun, and then called it good. Your Mastery images this finishing God. When you honor a commitment at cost, when you build something that will still be standing ten years from now, when you see a thing through — you are reflecting the faithful Creator who does not abandon his works.

Hebrews 12:2 describes Jesus as the one who "for the joy set before him endured the cross, despising the shame." To hold a long goal even through difficulty — this is not a merely human virtue. It is the reflection of a persevering God.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to let your diligence become an act of worship rather than an engine of identity. The parable of the talents ends with "Well done, good and faithful servant." Notice: faithful. Not maximally productive. Not most efficient. Faithful. Your calling is to do your work as one whose labor is received by a Master who sees and honors it — not to do your work as proof that you deserve a place at the table.

Jerry Bridges writes in <i>Who Am I?</i> that our identity must be rooted in Christ's finished work, not our own. For you, that is the specific invitation: let the work be an offering, held open-handed, finished but not clutched.

**The shadow for your pole**

High Mastery has two characteristic shadows. The first is works-righteousness — the quiet drift from "I am faithful" to "my faithfulness secures me." When diligence becomes the source of your sense of worth, your own failures become catastrophes rather than occasions for grace, and the person who doesn't meet your standard becomes a threat or a source of contempt rather than a neighbor.

The second shadow is more relational, and it shows up at the kitchen table and in the car and in the bedroom. You can be harder on others than love allows. The person who runs tight and finishes clean can lose patience with the spouse who works differently, the colleague who shows up late, the child who doesn't close the loop. Powlison would ask: "What do you do with the person who keeps interrupting your work?" What that person reveals in you is worth examining honestly.

**One gospel anchor**

"For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast." (Ephesians 2:8–9)

You were not saved by finishing well. You will not be kept by finishing well. The grace that meets you here is not a permission to be careless. It is a release from the exhausting labor of making your faithfulness mean too much.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that they can trust you — that what you say will happen, happens. This is a profound gift in a marriage. They may also notice that you can turn the energy of your standards toward them: tracking what they haven't finished, feeling the discomfort of their different pace, communicating (without meaning to) that your way of managing life is the right way. What they need from you is not lower standards. It is your willingness to let their faithfulness look different from yours.

### LOW Mastery

> **Mastery — LOW (below the 60th percentile)**
> Mastery is your wiring for effort and structure — and your wiring holds those things more loosely, which makes you available for things tight structure misses.
> Biblical anchor: "Commit your work to the Lord, and your plans will be established." (Proverbs 16:3)

The schedule changes — the meeting moves, the project pivots, the dinner plans fall through. Most people in the room tighten up, recalculate, feel the friction of having to start over. You just... adjust. Not because you're indifferent, but because you were never gripping the original plan that hard to begin with. You were already watching what was actually happening in front of you. That readiness is a real gift, even when it doesn't feel like one.

**What this wiring looks like**

You hold effort, structure, and goal-directed work more loosely than many people around you. That looseness is not sloth. It is a different orientation toward time, process, and completion. Rigid systems can feel like a cage that keeps you from responding to what is actually in front of you. You are more likely to work by the immediate pull of attention than by the predetermined plan — and that can mean you're wonderfully present with people in ways that the tightly scheduled person, always with one eye on the clock, simply cannot manage.

Think of the person at the church potluck who never signed up for anything but ends up being the one who noticed the elderly couple in the corner who couldn't find seats, and spent the next forty-five minutes with them, and those two people never forgot it. Or the one at the office who isn't the best at quarterly reports but is the one everyone goes to in the actual emergency, because they can think on their feet without the panic that comes from a rigid plan falling apart. That availability — for the present, for the actual, for the real person right now — is your gift.

**The imago Dei connection**

The God of Scripture is not only the God of completions. He is also the God of interruptions. Jesus was on his way somewhere when the woman with the issue of blood touched his cloak. He stopped (Luke 8:43–48). The whole story turns on that stop. In Acts 16, the apostles had a plan for their missionary route and the Spirit redirected it — twice (vv. 6–7). They went somewhere they hadn't planned to go and there was a church in Philippi.

Your capacity to hold plans loosely, to respond to the present, to be available for the thing that wasn't on the schedule — this images a God who is sovereign over the interruption as well as the plan. The Spirit blows where it will (John 3:8). There is something of that freedom in you.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to let your flexibility become a form of faithfulness rather than an escape from it. Proverbs 13:4 says "the soul of the sluggard craves and gets nothing." The warning is not against flexibility — it is against craving without doing, dreaming without committing. The discipline you need is not the discipline of a rigid system. It is the discipline of presence: "I will be here for this person, for this day, in this moment" — and meaning it.

Small faithfulness done consistently is still faithfulness. You don't need a five-year plan. You need to be the kind of person who keeps showing up to the things that matter, even without a system forcing you to.

**The shadow for your pole**

The shadow of your wiring is not primarily laziness. It is the substitution of good intentions for action — the cycle of beginning without completing, the slow erosion of trust in those who needed to count on you and eventually stopped.

Powlison's X-ray question is useful here: "What are you avoiding by staying unstructured?" Sometimes the looseness is genuine temperament. And sometimes it is protection — from failure, from evaluation, from the vulnerability of having actually tried and having the result be measurable. Bridges writes in <i>Who Am I?</i> that we are sometimes more comfortable talking about who we might be than committing to who we are becoming. The call for you is to risk completion. To say the thing, mean the thing, do the thing — and let it be seen.

**One gospel anchor**

"Commit your work to the Lord, and your plans will be established." (Proverbs 16:3)

You do not have to manufacture a system of self-discipline from within. What you need to do is bring what you have — your presence, your responsiveness, your willingness to try — and commit it. The Lord who receives that commitment does not require that it look like someone else's.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that you are not ruled by your calendar — that you can be fully present with them, flexible in a crisis, easy to redirect when something more important comes up. They may also notice moments when your looseness creates real difficulty: when the thing you said you'd do hasn't happened, when the appointment you forgot mattered to them. What they need is not for you to become a rigid planner. What they need is to know that when you say something matters to you, you mean it — and that you'll find your own way of honoring that.

## A — Animation

### HIGH Animation

> **Animation — HIGH (at or above the 60th percentile)**
> Animation is your wiring for people — the ease with which you move toward others and bring energy into a room.
> Biblical anchor: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news." (Isaiah 52:7)

You walk into the gathering and within ten minutes you know the name of the person standing alone near the snack table, you've started a conversation with the couple who looked like they might leave early, and somehow the whole corner of the room is a little warmer than it was. You didn't plan that. You didn't even notice you were doing it. That's just what happens when you show up. That is a gift, and you should know it.

**What this wiring looks like**

You are energized by people rather than depleted by them — not necessarily because every social situation is easy, but because something in you moves toward. You make connections quickly. Warmth comes naturally. You tend to create the kind of atmosphere where others feel invited in rather than assessed.

Think of the family in the neighborhood who always has people over — not formal dinner parties, just the door that's usually open and the kitchen that always seems to have food — and everyone ends up there without quite being able to explain why. Or the person at work who somehow knows everyone's name by the end of the first week and who, a month in, is the person new employees look for when they're lost. That natural gravity toward people, that ease in the social current, is your Animation.

You probably initiate: conversations, plans, decisions, the gathering itself. People look to you not because you claimed authority but because you moved when others were still deciding.

**The imago Dei connection**

The God of the Bible does not remain in isolation. The Trinity is, at its heart, an eternal communion — Father, Son, and Spirit in mutual love and delight. And the whole arc of Scripture is a God who moves toward: toward Abraham in Ur, toward Israel in Egypt, toward the woman at the well, toward the prodigal at the end of the road. Your Animation images this coming-toward of God. When you initiate, when you create the conditions in which others can show up, when your presence turns a collection of individuals into something that feels like a community — you are doing something the relational God made you to do.

The Herald of Isaiah 52:7 runs with good news. News is meant to be shared. You are built to share it.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to ensure that your engagement is truly for others rather than a stage for yourself. High Animation brings great gifts to every room it enters — but rooms have other people in them who carry things that need to be heard, not only received. Your calling includes the discipline of the still, small voice: learning when the most powerful thing you can do is go quiet and let others be heard.

Priscilla and Aquila in Acts 18 were people of real relational initiative. But when Apollos needed correction, they took him "aside" (v. 26) — privately, carefully, without performance. Your warmth can do what their warmth did, when you've learned to use it quietly.

**The shadow for your pole**

The shadow of high Animation is the crowd that becomes an audience, the warmth that curdles into a need for warmth, the energy that slowly becomes a demand for energy. Welch's diagnosis in <i>When People Are Big and God Is Small</i> applies here: when our happiness becomes too dependent on the presence and approval of others, we've allowed people to become too large and God too small.

A person of high Animation can become, without quite intending it, someone who subtly shapes every room toward their own comfort — who goes quiet or brittle when the response they expected doesn't come. It shows up at dinner when no one is engaging the story you're telling. It shows up in marriage when a quiet evening feels like rejection. The shadow is not your openness to people. It is when people become the oxygen you cannot breathe without.

**One gospel anchor**

"For God did not give us a spirit of fear, but of power and love and self-control." (2 Timothy 1:7)

The power and love in you are given, not generated. You do not have to produce your own energy. The Spirit who gives life to all things is the source of yours. When you are depleted, you are invited to receive rather than perform.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that life with you is alive — that you bring people together, that you make things happen, that the room changes when you walk in. They may also notice that you can take up more than your share of the air — that your energy, at full volume, can crowd out the quieter things they have to offer. What they need from you is not less energy. It is the willingness to sometimes let the room stay quiet long enough for them to fill it.

### LOW Animation

> **Animation — LOW (below the 60th percentile)**
> Animation is your wiring for people and social energy — and your wiring runs toward depth and quiet rather than breadth and volume.
> Biblical anchor: "He wakens my ear to listen." (Isaiah 50:4)

The party is crowded and loud. People are moving in every direction. An hour and a half in, you have had one conversation — and it was a real one. By the end of it you know something true about that person, and they know something true about you. The people who spent the evening making twenty-five shallow connections will not remember most of them tomorrow. You will remember yours for years. That is not a consolation prize. That is what you were made for.

**What this wiring looks like**

You tend toward depth over breadth, toward fewer connections honored carefully rather than many connections gathered quickly. Social situations may cost you something — not because you dislike people, but because being present in a crowd takes a kind of effort that needs recovery time. Large gatherings exhaust you. One-on-one conversations give you life.

Think of the friend you always call when something hard is happening — not the one who will have the most to say or who will fill the silence fastest, but the one who will actually stay. The one who calls back. The one whose listening makes you feel less alone, not because of what they said, but because you can tell they were really there. That capacity for deep, unhurried attention is your gift to everyone who gets to receive it.

You are more comfortable being known than being noticed. And you may have felt, in a culture that prizes loudness, that your quietness was a liability. It is not. It is often where the deepest thinking happens.

**The imago Dei connection**

God spoke the world into being — but he also met Elijah in the still small voice. Not in the wind, not in the earthquake, not in the fire (1 Kings 19:11–13). The Word that was in the beginning was also the Word that sat quietly with Mary at his feet. That same Word wept at Lazarus's tomb before saying anything. The incarnate God was not always performing.

Isaiah 50:4 describes the gift of "the tongue of the learned" — but notice how the verse begins: "He wakens me morning by morning, wakens my ear to listen." The listening comes first. Your wiring is built for that first movement — attentive, unhurried, receptive.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to bring what you carry into the room, even when it feels risky. Low Animation does not mean low impact. It means your impact comes differently, and often more permanently. What you have thought through with care, what you have listened to at depth, what you have held quietly until it became clear — the body of Christ needs that.

John the Baptist was not a man of crowds. He was a voice in the wilderness (John 1:23). But he was a voice. He spoke, even from the margins, even to people who might not immediately understand. The shadow of your wiring is the tendency to keep your gifts to yourself — not from selfishness, but from a reticence that can masquerade as humility. The call is to speak, from wherever you are.

**The shadow for your pole**

The shadow of your wiring is not shyness — it is withdrawal that hardens into withholding. The richness of your interior life can become a walled garden that others are never invited into. You become present in body and absent in everything that matters.

There is also a subtler shadow: the quiet judgment of those whose Animation runs high — the private sense that their warmth is shallow and your depth is superior. Powlison observes in <i>Seeing with New Eyes</i> that we often make a virtue of our native inclinations and a sin of others'. Your depth is real. But the animated person in the room has gifts you need, and the body of Christ is incomplete without both of you in it.

**One gospel anchor**

"For we are the body of Christ, and individually members of it." (1 Corinthians 12:27)

You were not made to be a whole body by yourself. The parts of the body that are less visible are not less necessary. What you carry in the quiet — the listening, the depth, the carefully prepared word — is needed by the parts of the body that move faster than they think.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that you are one of the few people who truly listens — that you remember what they said, that you hold their weight without rushing to fix it, that conversations you do initiate go somewhere real. They may also notice that they sometimes cannot find you — that you withdraw when you're overwhelmed, and that your silence can feel like absence even when you are sitting right there. What they need is not for you to become loud. It is for you to let them know where you are.

## G — Grace-bearing

### HIGH Grace-bearing

> **Grace-bearing — HIGH (at or above the 60th percentile)**
> Grace-bearing is your wiring for the wellbeing of others — the capacity to feel what they feel and the instinct to put their needs ahead of your own.
> Biblical anchor: "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth." (John 1:14)

Someone at your table — the holiday dinner, the after-church lunch, the Tuesday night small group — is struggling. They haven't said anything. No one else has noticed. But you noticed ten minutes ago, before the food was served, in something about the way they carried themselves when they walked in. And now you're making small adjustments: you positioned yourself closer, you asked the question that opened a door, you made sure they weren't left out of the conversation. You may not even be aware you did it. That is how you love people. That is your wiring.

**What this wiring looks like**

You feel the weight of others' pain as a real weight — not just an awareness of it, but something close to actually sharing it. You tend toward harmony. In conflict, your instinct is often to find the middle way, to hear both sides, to soften what is hard. Direct confrontation costs you something — not because you're weak, but because the rupture of a relationship feels like the rupture of something important.

Think of the person everyone calls when they've had hard news — not because that person will have the best advice or the most polished response, but because you know they'll actually be present. They won't make it about themselves. They'll just stay. Or the one at work who notices that the new employee is nervous and quietly does the thing that makes it less awkward, without anyone asking them to and without making a show of it. That is the Grace-bearing wiring at work.

You are the person people go to when they need to be heard without judgment. In a world that celebrates self-assertion, your gentleness can be mistaken for passivity. It is not. It is a different kind of strength.

**The imago Dei connection**

"God is love" (1 John 4:8). Not merely that God loves — though he does — but that love is constitutive of who he is. And the love Scripture calls his greatest commandment includes love toward neighbor (Matthew 22:37–39). Your Grace-bearing images the heart of God that moves toward the suffering, that refuses to be indifferent to the person in front of you, that treats another's humanity as genuinely precious.

Mark 1:41 tells us that Jesus was moved with compassion before he healed the leper. The compassion preceded the action. Your wiring works the same way — you feel first, then respond. That sequence is not a liability. It images a God who is not detached from the world he rules.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to make sure that your gentleness is governed by truth and not only by the desire for peace. John 1:14 says the Word came "full of grace and truth" — both, together, neither without the other. High Grace-bearing is a profound gift to any marriage, any small group, any friendship. But grace without truth eventually becomes something other than grace. It becomes the management of surfaces — a kindness that avoids the hard thing and leaves the person without what they actually needed.

Your calling includes the difficult kindness of honesty. The willingness to remain in relationship while still speaking what is real. To love the person enough to tell them the true thing, even when the true thing costs the peace.

**The shadow for your pole**

Welch names the shadow of your wiring with precision in <i>When People Are Big and God Is Small</i>: when the approval of others becomes so important that you will sacrifice truth and your own integrity to maintain it, you have allowed people to become too large in your world and God too small. The people-pleasing version of high Grace-bearing is not love — it is fear wearing the costume of love. It says "yes" when it should say "no." It keeps a peace that isn't peace. It avoids conflict not to protect the relationship but to protect itself from the discomfort of rupture.

The hard spiritual question for your pole is this: do you love the person in front of you, or do you love the way they feel about you?

**One gospel anchor**

"Am I now seeking the approval of man, or of God? Or am I trying to please man? If I were still trying to please man, I would not be a servant of Christ." (Galatians 1:10)

Your desire to honor and care for others is right and good. The anchor is to know whose approval finally matters. Not so that you become harsh — but so that your care for others flows from a secure place rather than from fear of their disapproval.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that you are one of the safest people they have ever known — that they can bring their worst to you and not be destroyed by it, that you will hold them without judgment, that you are genuinely for them. They may also notice, in harder seasons, that you sometimes tell them what they want to hear rather than what they need to hear — that your gentleness can become a kind of avoidance. What they need from you is not less warmth. It is the courage to love them enough to tell the truth.

### LOW Grace-bearing

> **Grace-bearing — LOW (below the 60th percentile)**
> Grace-bearing is your wiring for accommodation and yielding — and your wiring runs toward directness, plain speech, and the courage to hold your ground.
> Biblical anchor: "Speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ." (Ephesians 4:15)

The group has been circling the problem for forty minutes. People have been polite. No one has said the hard thing. You've been sitting with it, listening, and finally you say it — plainly, without softening it into incomprehensibility. The room goes quiet. Some people look uncomfortable. But three days later, two people pull you aside and say it was exactly what needed to be said. That is your gift. The Church often needs it more than it will admit.

**What this wiring looks like**

You lead with honesty rather than accommodation. Where others tend to yield, you tend to hold your ground. Where others might soften what they mean to protect a relationship, you tend to say what you mean and trust the relationship to withstand it. You are probably less easily manipulated than those who find conflict more costly. You see a strong-willed argument coming and you don't automatically fold. You feel the pull of justice before the pull of mercy — not because mercy is foreign to you, but because what strikes you first is what is true and what is right.

Think of the elder who, in the meeting where everyone else is deferring to a bad idea, says clearly and without drama: "I don't think this is right, and here's why." Or the friend who tells you, kindly but directly, what everyone else is thinking but no one else will say. Sometimes the truest person in the room is the one the room needed most. You know this. You've probably lived it.

**The imago Dei connection**

The prophets were not notable for their agreeableness. Elijah stood alone on Carmel and told four hundred and fifty prophets that they were wrong (1 Kings 18). John the Baptist told Herod that his marriage was unlawful (Mark 6:18). Paul, when Peter compromised the gospel at Antioch, "opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned" (Galatians 2:11). These were not failures of love. They were expressions of a particular kind of love — one that takes the truth and the other person seriously enough to say the hard thing.

The God who is love is also the God who is truth. Sometimes the most loving act is to refuse to agree with what is false or harmful. Your willingness to hold your ground, to name what is real, to stay in the room and disagree — this images the God who did not smooth over the contradiction between his holiness and our sin but addressed it at infinite cost.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to ensure that your directness is governed by love for the person rather than love for being right. 1 Corinthians 13:1 warns that even speech with the tongues of men and angels, without love, is only noise. The prophetic tradition is always anchored in care for the people the prophet is addressing — even the hardest words are spoken because the prophet cannot bear to watch people go the wrong way. Jeremiah wept. He wasn't indifferent; he was heartbroken.

The call for your wiring is to let the truth you carry be warm — to remember that the person across from you is not an argument to be won but a soul for whom Christ died. The truth doesn't change. The way you carry it into the room is always a choice.

**The shadow for your pole**

R.D. Jones, in <i>Uprooting Anger</i>, describes the shadow of your wiring with care: righteous directness becomes unrighteous harshness when it uses truth as a weapon rather than as a gift. The person who is constitutionally direct can become, over time, contemptuous — not just unimpressed by others' opinions, but dismissive of others as persons. The prophetic gift turns into a courtroom where you are always the prosecuting attorney and the verdict is always the same.

Powlison names it this way: when I treat every interaction as a chance to be right rather than a chance to love, I have traded the servant-heart of the gospel for the self-satisfaction of my own correctness. Your directness is a genuine gift. The shadow is directness that has forgotten the person it is meant to serve.

**One gospel anchor**

"Speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ." (Ephesians 4:15)

Truth without love is not the gospel's way. Neither is love without truth. The two belong together — and in your wiring, truth comes easily. The invitation is to let love carry it.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that life with you is honest — that they always know where they stand, that you won't let important things go unsaid, that your word is reliable and your opinions are real. They may also notice that they sometimes feel the weight of your directness more than they can carry at a given moment — that the way you say true things can leave marks that outlast the conversation. What they need from you is not less honesty. It is the softness of delivery that lets them actually receive what only you have the courage to say.

## O — Ortho-emotion

### HIGH Ortho-emotion

> **Ortho-emotion — HIGH (at or above the 60th percentile)**
> Ortho-emotion is your wiring for emotional steadiness — the capacity to stay present and grounded when the world around you is unsettled.
> Biblical anchor: "I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother." (Psalm 131:2)

The phone call comes. There's been an accident, or a diagnosis, or a marriage is in trouble. The room fills with a kind of electricity — people don't know what to do with their hands, voices start climbing. And there you are, steady. Not cold. Not indifferent. You feel it. But you don't come apart. You ask the next right question, or you just sit there and let the weight settle, and somehow your being-there starts to do something to the room. People don't always thank you for this. They may not even realize you did it. But you are one of the ways God shows up in the hard hour.

**What this wiring looks like**

You experience the world through a more even emotional register. You are not typically shaken by what shakes others, and you tend to return to equilibrium relatively quickly after stress. Your emotional baseline runs closer to settled than turbulent.

Think of the ER nurse who has seen everything and doesn't stop being kind, doesn't panic, doesn't make the patient feel more afraid by their own distress — who just does the next thing with a steady hand. Or the parent who, when the toddler is melting down at the grocery store and everyone else is staring, doesn't escalate — just gets lower, speaks quietly, and the whole situation softens. That capacity to not amplify the anxiety already in the room is your gift. You bring it everywhere you go.

You probably don't carry the emotional residue of a conflict long after it's resolved. You're not prone to catastrophizing. You don't tend to read a hard day as evidence of a hard life. Emotions, for you, are visitors — they arrive, they have their say, and they leave.

**The imago Dei connection**

The peace of God "which surpasses all understanding" (Philippians 4:7) is not the absence of feeling. It is a settled orientation that holds through circumstances. God is described in Scripture as a refuge, a fortress, a rock — images of stability that protect rather than suppress. Psalm 131 gives us a picture of the quieted soul: "I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother." Your emotional steadiness images this settled God — not the God who is indifferent to the world's pain, but the God whose presence does not fluctuate with the weather.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to let your steadiness be available to others — not held in reserve, not hoarded for yourself, but extended as a gift. Paul Tripp writes in <i>New Morning Mercies</i> that we are sometimes most blind to our gifts precisely because they come so naturally. Your steadiness may feel unremarkable to you. To the person in crisis beside you, it is remarkable.

The honest challenge that comes with it: the Psalms are full of people who named their anguish before God — who wept and raged and despaired openly (Psalm 22, 88, 130). That emotional range may feel foreign to you. The Spirit may be inviting you to widen your vocabulary of feeling — not because you are broken, but because the full range of human experience is sometimes asked of us in order to sit with others who are drowning. To mourn with those who mourn, you have to know what mourning is.

**The shadow for your pole**

The shadow of your wiring is not stoicism — it is the slow substitution of emotional unavailability for emotional strength. Steadiness can harden, over time, into numbness. The person who never falls apart can become the person who never mourns with those who mourn (Romans 12:15) — present in the room during someone else's grief, perfectly composed, and therefore somehow absent. There in body. Nowhere in spirit.

Welch names a related pattern in <i>Shame Interrupted</i>: sometimes what looks like peace is actually a deep internal world that has learned not to surface. The question for your pole is not "are you steady?" — you are. The question is whether your steadiness is alive and warm, or whether it has become a kind of emotional distance dressed in the language of faith.

**One gospel anchor**

"Weep with those who weep." (Romans 12:15)

The steady person's challenge is not to stop being steady. It is to be moved, within their steadiness — to let the sorrow of others actually enter. The God who keeps your soul in perfect peace is also the God who wept at Lazarus's tomb.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that you are a calm harbor — that they can come to you in the storm and find someone who is not being tossed about. This is a profound gift. They may also notice, in deep seasons of grief or fear, that they can't quite find you emotionally. Your calm can feel like somewhere they can't reach. What they need is not for you to perform distress. It is for your steadiness to be warm rather than sealed, so they know you are with them and not merely beside them.

### LOW Ortho-emotion

> **Ortho-emotion — LOW (below the 60th percentile)**
> Ortho-emotion is your wiring for emotional life — and your wiring runs deep, feeling the world at full volume, like the Psalmists who held nothing back from God.
> Biblical anchor: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1)

You finish a hard conversation and you can't just move on. The words are still turning over in you hours later, maybe days later. You feel it in your chest, in your sleep, in the way the room tastes different after something unsettled. Some people wonder how you get through life carrying this much. What they don't understand is that this same wiring — this deep, full-register feeling — is also how you love people, how you pray, how you notice things others walk right past. The depth that makes the hard things hard is the same depth that makes you who you are.

**What this wiring looks like**

You feel things at greater amplitude than many people around you. The highs are high. The lows are lower than most people's, and the transitions between them can be swift and sometimes disorienting. Conflict takes time to metabolize. A hard conversation can live in your body for days. You are probably more alert to threat and to what-could-go-wrong than your steadier counterparts — not because you lack faith, but because your emotional system is calibrated for sensitivity.

Think of the person in the Psalms who cannot fake it before God — who writes "How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13:1) and means every word of it. Or the friend who, when you are grieving, doesn't hand you a platitude — they sit with you in it, because they know what it is to sit in something, and they aren't afraid of the dark. That emotional fluency, that willingness to be present to the weight of things, is your gift. It is a ministry.

You are probably the first person in a room to notice that something is wrong — in a relationship, in a group, in yourself. What you pick up, others often miss entirely.

**The imago Dei connection**

The Psalms were written by people who felt everything. Psalm 22 opens with abandonment: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — and it doesn't pretend otherwise. Psalm 88 ends in darkness without resolution. Lamentations is a whole book of raw grief. The fact that God gave his people an entire library of emotionally honest poetry, and then put it at the center of Scripture — that is not an accident.

The God who "knows our frame" and "remembers that we are dust" (Psalm 103:14) made some of his image-bearers to feel his world with particular acuity. Your sensitivity to pain — your own and others' — is not a malfunction. It is a form of knowledge. You know things about suffering that only the suffering know. The Church is richer for your presence in it. Not in spite of your feeling everything. Because of it.

**The calling for your pole**

The Spirit's invitation to you is to offer your emotional richness as a gift to the body, not to bear it as a private burden. The capacity to weep, to mourn, to feel deeply — this is exactly what Paul means when he says "mourn with those who mourn" (Romans 12:15). You don't have to learn to mourn. You already know how. Your calling is to let that knowing be consecrated and offered.

Tripp writes in <i>New Morning Mercies</i> that "your emotions are a grace, given so that you can feel what you should feel about what matters." The world is broken. Some things should produce anguish. You are wired to feel that truthfully — and that truthfulness is a ministry when it is submitted to the Spirit rather than managed in isolation.

**The shadow for your pole**

Your wiring has two characteristic shadows. The first is the equation of strong feeling with accurate perception. Not every wave of anxiety is a word from God. Not every fear is a prophecy. The intensity you experience can make it genuinely difficult to distinguish what you are feeling from what is actually true. Powlison's question in <i>Making All Things New</i> is essential here: "What is the story your feelings are telling, and is that story actually true?" Your feelings are real. They are not always right.

The second shadow is what Welch names in <i>Shame Interrupted</i>: when emotional turbulence becomes the lens through which every experience is filtered, the person can become the center of their own universe — not out of selfishness, but out of the exhaustion of simply managing the interior. The dinner that was supposed to be about the family becomes about managing your anxiety. The marriage conversation that was supposed to be about your spouse becomes about what you're feeling. The call is to let the feelings speak without letting them govern.

**One gospel anchor**

"Cast all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you." (1 Peter 5:7)

The invitation is not to stop feeling. It is to bring the full weight of what you feel to the One who is large enough to hold it. He is not alarmed by the force of your interior life. He made it, and he holds it.

**What your partner will notice**

Your partner will notice that you are alive to them — that when they hurt, you really feel it; that you bring warmth and intensity and a depth of emotional presence that many people never experience from anyone. They may also notice that there are seasons when your interior weather becomes the weather of the whole house. Your anxiety or sadness or frustration can color the atmosphere in ways that exhaust both of you. What they need is not for you to feel less. It is for you to know the difference between what is yours to feel and what is yours to release — and to practice, with grace, the discipline of not making every strong feeling a shared emergency.
