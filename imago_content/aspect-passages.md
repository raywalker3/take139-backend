# Aspect Passages

The five IMAGO domains give you the broad landscape of how you are wired. The ten aspects give you the finer texture beneath that landscape — two per domain, each one a specific way the broader pattern shows up in the details of daily life. Think of the domains as the main channels and the aspects as the particular current running through each one. You do not need to master the vocabulary. You just need to recognize yourself.

---

## I — Imagination

### Artistry (I1)

> **Artistry** — how your eyes and ears engage with beauty
> A sub-trait within the Imagination domain

There is a woman in the congregation who always notices the flowers — not to remark on the effort, but because something in her actually stops when she sees them. She will stand in a doorway for an extra three seconds when the afternoon light falls a certain way across the floor. On a road trip, she is the one who wants to pull over for the view even when you're already late. Nobody had to teach her this. Her nervous system was simply built to receive the sensory world as a source of meaning.

At the high end, this is you. You notice what others walk past. A slant of morning light through a curtain stops you — not because you're trying to be poetic but because your brain actually registers it as significant. You have strong opinions about music: not just genre preference, but chord changes, the way a lyric lands, the silence before the last note. You probably have opinions about the design of things — the font on a menu, the way a book is laid out, the way a room feels when you first walk in. You return to the same song ten times because something in it names what you cannot otherwise say. You absorb atmosphere. A film can stay with you for days because you were not just watching it but receiving it. This is the wiring that Exodus 31 describes in Bezalel — a craftsman whom God filled with his Spirit for the work of making beautiful things. The text does not say Bezalel was technically competent. It says he was filled with "ability and intelligence, with knowledge and all craftsmanship" — perception, not just production. That is the Artistry wiring.

At the low end, you relate to the world more through function and concept than through sensory texture. A painting is a painting. A room is a room. You are not indifferent to beauty exactly — but it does not pull at you the way it pulls at others, and you don't feel like you're missing anything when it doesn't. Your pleasure tends to come from a well-constructed argument, a solved problem, or work well done rather than from an aesthetic experience. You can work happily in an uninspiring office, attend a functional church service without needing it to be atmospheric, and move on from a meal without analyzing the flavors. This is not a gap in your wiring. It is a kind of freedom: you are not held hostage to the setting. You bring the same quality of attention to a fluorescent-lit conference room that others can only manage when everything feels right. There is a person in every useful committee who does not get distracted by how the slides look — and that person tends to be you.

Pastoral note: Both of these people are made in the image of a God who called his creation good — and meant it aesthetically as well as morally. The high-Artistry person images God's capacity to fill the world with beauty and to delight in it. The low-Artistry person images God's capacity to act, to decide, to build without needing the setting to cooperate first. Neither is more spiritual. The Spirit fills a Bezalel with artistic perception and fills a Nehemiah with gritty project management. The body of Christ needs both.

### Intellect (I2)

> **Intellect** — how drawn you are to ideas, argument, and abstract thinking
> A sub-trait within the Imagination domain

There is a man in every small group who cannot let a question rest. Someone asks an offhand question about predestination — or prayer, or why bad things happen — and he is still at it twenty minutes later, not because he is trying to dominate the conversation but because something in him genuinely cannot leave a question at the surface. He has read about this. He has opinions about the people who have read about this. He wants to follow it all the way down.

At the high end, ideas are not just useful to you — they are genuinely pleasurable. You read across fields because you find the connections between them interesting. You maintain opinions about things most people do not think about — philosophy of language, or the logic of a particular economic model, or the way a theological position works out three steps from its premise. You find argument enlivening rather than threatening: not hostile argument, but genuinely contested intellectual exchange where both sides have to think hard. You formulate ideas with precision. You feel something like dissatisfaction when a conversation stays too shallow, when a sermon stays too safe, when a meeting never surfaces the real question underneath the procedural one. At the grocery store, you are the person who starts reading the nutrition label and ends up thinking about subsidies. At church, you are the person who goes home and reads the commentary. In the Proverbs tradition, this is the wiring of those who "understand a matter before answering it" (Proverbs 18:13) — who know that the discipline of thinking hard is a form of love toward the truth.

At the low end, you are not less curious about the world. You are simply less pulled toward abstract or theoretical exploration as the entry point. You tend to prefer the concrete, the applicable, the relational. A forty-five-minute theological debate may leave you drained in the way a long hike might leave someone else drained — it is not that you can't do it, but it costs something you would rather spend elsewhere. You are often more energized by practical problem-solving, by working with your hands, by people, or by craft. In communities full of thinkers, you are frequently the one who asks, "Yes — but what do we actually do?" That question saves many otherwise brilliant groups from beautiful paralysis. The person who stops the discussion and calls for action is not missing something. They are contributing something the discussion alone cannot supply.

Pastoral note: God invites us to "come, let us reason together" (Isaiah 1:18) — and that same God reveals himself in burning bush and broken bread, in the sensory and particular. A mind that finds ideas thrilling is a gift. So is a mind that knows when to stop talking and start building. The person whose Intellect runs high is called to bring rigor and depth to the community. The person whose Intellect runs low is called to bring the community back to earth before it disappears into its own cleverness. Both callings are real.

## M — Mastery

### Industriousness (M1)

> **Industriousness** — how strongly you are driven to begin, sustain, and finish your work
> A sub-trait within the Mastery domain

Picture the person in any volunteer ministry who shows up every week without being reminded, completes the prep work before the meeting, and — three months after everyone else has moved on to the next thing — is still following through on the thing they said they would do. Nobody had to create a system to manage them. They are, in a quiet and functional way, the reason the thing keeps running.

At the high end, this is how you move through the world. You wake up oriented toward what needs to be done. You carry a running list — mental or written, it doesn't matter, because it is always running — and you feel something genuinely uncomfortable when you sense you are falling behind. You are the one who remembers that you promised to send the article, who shows up early because being on time feels late, who finishes the project that other people started enthusiastically and then quietly set down. Half-done work bothers you in a way that is difficult to explain to people who do not share the wiring. A half-unpacked suitcase, a half-answered email, a half-finished plan: these are not just minor inconveniences — they sit at the edge of your attention until they are resolved. In a congregation, in a workplace, in a family, high-Industriousness people are often the ones things actually get done through. They are not always the visionaries. But they are often the reason the vision becomes real. This is the wiring behind Matthew 25:21 — the servant who received five talents and came back with ten. Not because they were clever or ambitious, but because they were faithful with what they were given.

At the low end, you are not lazy in any morally freighted sense. You simply carry less of the internal pressure that drives sustained effort through long stretches of undramatic work. You may find it easy to begin things and harder to finish them. You may have excellent bursts of energy and real difficulty sustaining effort across weeks of ordinary maintenance. You are probably more spontaneous than your high-Industriousness counterpart, more comfort-tolerant, and more genuinely present-tense in your orientation — you are not always anxious about the next task, which means you are sometimes just here for this one. These can be real gifts. You are less likely to be enslaved to productivity, more able to rest without guilt, and sometimes more attentive to the person in front of you because you are not already thinking about the next thing. What you may need are structures, commitments, or people that supply the external pull your internal wiring does not.

Pastoral note: The gospel does not promise that all of us will be driven strivers, and it does not call all of us to Herculean output. It calls all of us to faithfulness — which looks different depending on how we are made. What it does promise is that we are loved not for what we produce but because we belong to Christ. The person whose Industriousness runs high may need that truth more urgently than the person whose runs low, because the temptation to confuse faithfulness with productivity is especially sharp when productivity comes easily.

### Orderliness (M2)

> **Orderliness** — how much you depend on structure, routine, and clear systems
> A sub-trait within the Mastery domain

There are two people in every office kitchen. One of them cannot leave without wiping down the counter. The other genuinely does not notice the counter. Neither is wrong about the counter. They are simply wired differently about what the counter means.

At the high end, you know where things are — and you notice immediately when they have moved. Your desk may not look minimalist to the outside eye, but it has a logic you could explain, and you feel the difference when that logic is disturbed. You build routines that hold your life in shape: a consistent morning sequence, a place for the keys, an agenda for the meeting with a realistic time estimate beside each item. You do your best thinking in an organized environment, and transitions come easier when you know what to expect. When life gets genuinely chaotic — a move, a medical crisis, a season when the schedule will not hold still — you feel it not just as inconvenience but as an unsettledness that takes real effort to work through. That is not weakness. It is information: you do your best work when the structure holds, and that deserves to be honored rather than apologized for. The God who designed the tabernacle with precise measurements, who set the sun and moon in their courses, who calls his people to "let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40) — this God understands what it means to need the structure to hold.

At the low end, you live comfortably in environments that would make your high-Orderliness counterpart tense. A messy desk does not register as a problem. An unscheduled afternoon is not a gap to be filled but an opening. A plan that shifts at the last minute is just what happened — you adapt, and you are generally good at it. You tend to be flexible and responsive: you work with what is actually in front of you rather than grieving the departure from what was planned. This makes you excellent in fluid situations, emergencies, or creative work where improvisation matters more than protocol. Some of your best thinking happens in apparent disarray. What you can struggle with is the maintenance work — the administrative follow-through, the remembered detail, the sustained system that holds over months. You may need a person or a tool to supply the structure your internal wiring does not generate naturally.

Pastoral note: Order is not an end in itself, but it is a servant of faithfulness. The keeper-of-order and the adapter-in-chaos are both tending something real. One tends the reliability that lets others rest in a predictable world. The other tends the flexibility that keeps the community responsive when the predictable world breaks down. Both are needed. The body of Christ runs on structure and on the people who can hold it together when the structure gives way.

## A — Animation

### Enthusiasm (A1)

> **Enthusiasm** — how naturally warm and energizing you are in the company of others
> A sub-trait within the Animation domain

You know her immediately when she walks in. She makes eye contact before she has even crossed the room, remembers your name when you only met once, and has a laugh that makes the conversation feel like it just got lighter. She does not manage the room — she simply fills it with something good. She is the reason the new family did not sit alone on their third Sunday, because she spotted them before the greeter with the lanyard did.

At the high end, you do not have to work at warmth. It is how you move. You are genuinely curious about the lives of people you have just met — not as a technique, but because you find people actually interesting. You laugh readily and without embarrassment. Social situations tend to energize you rather than drain you, and you are often the one who lingers at the end of a gathering because something in you does not want it to end. You probably have a large number of people you genuinely consider friends, and you carry a running awareness of what is happening in their lives. At the church potluck, you are already talking to the person standing alone by the vegetable tray before anyone else has finished serving themselves. At the hospital, you are the visitor who has a reputation for making other people's rooms feel lighter. This is something of the warmth of Priscilla and Aquila, who opened their home as if hospitality were the most natural thing in the world (Romans 16:5) — because for them, it was.

At the low end, you are not cold. Your warmth is simply slower to show, more reserved, more selective — and not less real for being those things. You tend to care deeply about specific people while finding large groups taxing. A party drains you in a way that a long one-on-one conversation does not. You are not the person who lights up a room, but you are often the person who makes one person in it feel genuinely heard — which is its own irreplaceable gift. Long-term friends discover unexpected depth in you precisely because you never led with performance. You are steady in relationship in a way that the high-Enthusiasm person sometimes is not: you show up the same way on Tuesday that you showed up three months ago, which is what people who are actually struggling tend to need most.

Pastoral note: God's love is unstinting and he runs toward the returning son from a long way off (Luke 15:20). The high-Enthusiasm person knows something of that impulse in their bones. The low-Enthusiasm person knows something of the quieter love that simply stays. The body of Christ needs both — the one who makes the first connection and the one who keeps it long after the excitement of new friendship has worn away.

### Assertiveness (A2)

> **Assertiveness** — how naturally you move toward leadership, decision, and influence
> A sub-trait within the Animation domain

There is a moment in every meeting when the conversation has been circling for twenty minutes and everyone is waiting for someone to say the thing that names where they actually are and what needs to happen next. Some people wait to be asked. Some people feel the pull to just say it. Assertiveness is what determines which of those you are.

At the high end, you do not wait to be asked. When a group is drifting, you feel the pull to name what needs to happen next — and you generally do. You have strong opinions and you are willing to say them, often before you have checked whether the room is ready. In conflict or high-stakes situations, you tend to move toward resolution rather than wait for someone else to manage it. You carry a natural authority that others often feel before they can explain why they defer to you. You are not always comfortable with consensus as a method: you can see that sometimes the most caring thing is to make a call and move. In a world that needs people willing to lead, to push, to act, this is a genuine gift — and it is a particular gift in the church, where leadership has sometimes been so hedged that nobody moves. This is something of Nehemiah's wiring: he surveyed the broken wall in the dark, gathered the people, and said "Come, let us build" (Nehemiah 2:17-18) — without waiting for a committee to ratify what was obvious. He did not always wait for consensus. That turned out to be exactly right.

At the low end, you are not passive. You simply carry less of the pull toward influence and direction. You tend to be genuinely comfortable following, contributing without directing, and letting others take the decision when the decision is not yours to make. You notice the costs of dominance more clearly than the high-Assertiveness person does — the conversation that got steamrolled, the voice that went unheard — and you tend to be willing to yield on things that feel less essential. You can be an excellent collaborator in environments where position is not the point. When you do speak up — because you are moved, not because you need to be heard — what you say tends to carry unusual weight precisely because you do not say it often. The person who rarely speaks in the meeting is the person everyone listens to when they finally do.

Pastoral note: The cross makes both postures imaginable as holy. Christ, who had every authority, took a towel (John 13:4). The same Christ drove money-changers out of the temple with a whip he made himself (John 2:15). Assertiveness is a tool. So is restraint. The wisdom is knowing when each is an act of love.

## G — Grace-bearing

### Compassion (G1)

> **Compassion** — how readily you feel what others are feeling and move toward their pain
> A sub-trait within the Grace-bearing domain

There is a person in every congregation who calls when someone hasn't shown up two Sundays in a row — not because they were assigned to, but because they noticed, and because the noticing immediately became a weight they needed to act on. They are the ones who remember, three weeks after the surgery, to ask how recovery is actually going. They cry at other people's good news. They are not performing any of this. Their emotional life is simply porous to the people around them.

At the high end, this is you. You carry others' emotional weight naturally, and it is not something you choose to do — it simply arrives. When a friend describes a hard week, your first move is not toward the solution. It is toward the person. You notice when something is off in someone's face before they have said a word. You cry at films, sometimes at the news, sometimes at the right line in a hymn — not because you are easily overwhelmed but because the emotional content of the world actually enters you rather than bouncing off. You tend to be among the first to check on the person who didn't come back to church, the coworker who has seemed quiet for a week, the neighbor whose lights have been off. In a hospital room or a grief gathering, people feel something different when you are present — not that you say the right thing (you may not) but that you are actually with them in it. This is the wiring behind Romans 12:15: "weep with those who weep" — not as a discipline for you, but as a first impulse the rest of us are trying to cultivate.

At the low end, you are not unfeeling. Your first response to someone's distress is more likely to be practical, analytical, or problem-solving than immediately empathic. You may care deeply about a person's wellbeing while not being especially drawn into their emotional state in the moment. In many situations this is exactly what is needed: you can stay clearheaded when others are overwhelmed, offer a practical step when everyone else is just emoting, and make decisions that require some distance from the feeling of the situation. In the grief room, you are the one who quietly figures out who needs a ride home and makes sure they have one. You may not cry at the funeral, but you will be the person who calls three months later to check whether the hard part is still hard — because you thought about it practically, not emotionally, and you knew enough to know the calendar does not end grief.

Pastoral note: Compassion in Scripture is never a feeling that stays private — it moves. The Good Samaritan saw, felt, and then crossed the road (Luke 10:33). The high-Compassion person and the low-Compassion person can both be instruments of that mercy. God uses the one who aches alongside people and the one who stays clear-headed enough to act. Both are needed. The body of Christ is the whole movement: feeling and doing together.

### Courtesy (G2)

> **Courtesy** — how strongly you are wired to hold back from running over others
> A sub-trait within the Grace-bearing domain

This is not about manners. It is not about which fork to use or whether you say "please." What it tracks is something more structural: the degree to which a person is internally resistant to using their weight — conversational, relational, positional, emotional — to push past others and get their way. Two people can both say "thank you" and hold the door, and one of them will steamroll the whole meeting and the other will not. That difference lives here.

At the high end, you have a strong internal pull away from dominance. You are the person who, in a conversation, catches yourself about to interrupt and waits instead. You notice when you have more power in a room — more status, more confidence, more relational capital — and you feel the weight of that asymmetry. You would rather lose an argument than win it by forcing the other person to submit. You tend to have a deep intuitive resistance to manipulation: using relationships as instruments, leveraging position, winning by attrition. You ask permission when others assume. In decision-making, you are the one who wants to make sure the quieter voices have actually been heard before the group decides. The language of Philippians 2:3 — "count others more significant than yourselves" — is not a discipline you have to work toward. It is a natural orientation you live from, often without naming it. In leadership, this is a profound gift. The world has no shortage of people willing to use power. It is shorter on people who feel its weight and use it carefully.

At the low end, you carry less of that internal resistance to dominance. You are more willing to push through when you believe you are right — to use your social capital freely, to push past procedural hesitation, to be comfortable with asymmetric influence when the situation seems to call for it. This is not predatory. It simply means you do not feel the same pull to yield, to defer, or to make room. In the right hands, this makes you an effective advocate and a vigorous leader — the person who gets things done in systems that resist change, who will push the right idea past the wrong resistance. The danger is that the same wiring can express itself as dismissiveness, as a pattern of using people as means rather than ends, as a quiet assumption that your judgment should prevail. This is where Welch's counsel lands: when the people around you become instruments of your agenda rather than persons to be honored, you have moved from strength into sin.

Pastoral note: Christ had all authority. He used it by washing feet (John 13:4-5). Every person, regardless of their natural wiring, is called toward that posture — not passivity, but power exercised in service. The high-Courtesy person offers the rest of us a living picture of what that feels like from the inside. The low-Courtesy person is called to cultivate the discipline of making room, not because yielding is their natural gear, but because love sometimes requires it anyway.

## O — Ortho-emotion

### Sensitivity (O1)

> **Sensitivity** — how alert your nervous system is to threat, worry, and emotional weight
> A sub-trait within the Ortho-emotion domain

There is a person who, after a difficult conversation, will replay it in their mind for the rest of the day. They noticed the slight change in tone at the end, wondered what it meant, imagined two or three scenarios in which it meant something they didn't want it to mean. By evening they will have worked through most of those scenarios and will probably have a sense of what was actually happening — but the replaying itself was not something they chose. It is simply how their nervous system processes the world.

At the high end, your emotional life is alert in a way that most people's is not. You rehearse conversations before they happen, revisit them after they're over, and carry the weight of possible consequences more readily than those around you. Your nervous system has a strong pull toward risk-awareness: you tend to see what could go wrong, to carry a background hum of concern that others do not seem to be tuned to. You may struggle with worry in extended seasons. You may experience sadness that arrives without an obvious external cause. Your confidence in social situations may fluctuate in ways that are hard to explain to people who move through rooms without that fluctuation. None of this is a character flaw. It is wiring. And it is the wiring of the Psalms — Psalm 6, Psalm 22, Psalm 88 — the honest tradition of people who felt the full weight of what the world can do to a soul and who brought that feeling before God rather than suppressing it. David was not broken when he cried out "How long, O Lord?" He was honest. High-Sensitivity people tend to be among the most honest people in a community, because they cannot easily pretend everything is fine when it is not. They also tend to be unusually attentive to others' pain, because they know from the inside what it costs.

At the low end, you move through difficulty with relative ease. Setbacks do not tend to stick to you. You bounce back from hard conversations without much rumination, tend toward confidence in social situations, and do not spend much mental energy rehearsing what might go wrong. When hard things happen, you process them and return to baseline without the extended internal weather that others experience. This is not the same as being shallow. It is a genuine form of resilience that the people around you depend on — you can be present with someone else's pain without being capsized by it, and you can keep going after losses that would slow others down for weeks. Where to pay attention: you can sometimes underestimate what difficult seasons cost the people around you, or move past shared pain faster than those people are ready to move. The low-Sensitivity person who assumes others are as ready to move on as they are can leave people feeling unseen.

Pastoral note: Neither wiring is more faithful. The person who feels everything deeply is not more spiritual than the person who processes loss quickly. Both need the anchor of Christ, who was himself a man of sorrows (Isaiah 53:3) and who promises a peace that passes understanding to those who bring their anxiety to him (Philippians 4:7). The invitation is the same for both. What the receiving looks like is different — and that is allowed.

### Steadiness (O2)

> **Steadiness** — how long your fuse is when things go wrong or people frustrate you
> A sub-trait within the Ortho-emotion domain

There is a man who, when his teenage son slams the door, does not slam anything back. He does not go cold. He sits with it for a moment, and then he goes and knocks on the door. The neighbor who cut him off in traffic yesterday — he has not thought about it since yesterday. His wife knows what his bad days look like, and they are not frightening. He is not performing calm. It is genuinely how he is built.

At the high end, you do not flare easily. When things go wrong, your first response is to absorb rather than to react. You have a long fuse in conflict. You tend to be slow to raise your voice, quick to extend the benefit of the doubt, and capable of staying regulated in situations that pull others apart. In a tense meeting, in a hard marriage moment, in a parenting situation that is not going the direction anyone planned, you are the presence that keeps things from escalating further — not because nothing is happening inside you, but because you have a strong natural capacity to hold what you feel before you do anything with it. The people around you feel safer because they know what your bad day actually looks like. They can breathe differently when you are in the room. This is a picture of what Psalm 103:8 describes: God is "slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" — not because he lacks feeling, but because his love is not at the mercy of his frustration. The high-Steadiness person knows something of that texture from the inside.

At the low end, you are quicker to irritability, frustration, and emotional expression when things do not go as expected. This is different from the anxiety of high Sensitivity: this is not worry about the future, but reactivity in the present moment. Your feelings surface quickly and visibly. You may find that disappointment becomes sharpness before you have had time to process the gap. A bad morning can color the whole day in ways that you are aware of and yet cannot entirely prevent. A sharp word may be out before you have decided to say it. This is not the same as having a bad character — many people whose Steadiness runs low are deeply caring, and genuinely remorseful about the relational cost of their reactivity. The gap is not between what they value and what they do. The gap is between what they feel and how fast it moves to the surface. The work here is not primarily about wanting to be calmer. It is about building the tiny structures — a breath, a walk, a practiced pause — that create a moment of distance between feeling and action.

Pastoral note: R.D. Jones writes that anger is not sin — what we do with anger determines whether it becomes a weapon or a wound we bring to God. The high-Steadiness person still has anger. They carry it differently. Both kinds of people need the gospel: the one who needs to soften what comes out too fast, and the one who needs to remember that being unaffected is not the same as being strong. Neither fuse length is the finish line. Christ is.
